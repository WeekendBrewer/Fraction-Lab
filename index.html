Labor market impacts of AI: A new measure and early evidence

Authors: Maxim Massenkoff and Peter McCrory

Economics Labor Market Artificial Intelligence

1. The Challenge of Measuring Disruption

The rapid diffusion of AI is generating a wave of research measuring its impacts on labor markets. But past approaches give reason for humility. A decade ago, forecasts identified a quarter of US jobs as vulnerable to offshorability, yet most maintained healthy growth.

This paper presents a new framework to measure how AI is affecting employment *before* meaningful effects emerge post-hoc. The impacts of AI might be less like the sudden shock of COVID-19 and more like the gradual integration of the internet or trade with China. By tracking this now, we can identify vulnerable jobs before displacement becomes overtly visible in aggregate unemployment data.

Key Findings at a Glance

  • AI is far from reaching its theoretical capability.
  • Occupations with higher observed exposure are projected by the BLS to grow less through 2034.
  • Highly exposed workers are more likely to be older, female, more educated, and higher-paid.
  • No systematic increase in unemployment for highly exposed workers since late 2022.
  • Suggestive evidence that hiring of younger workers (22-25) has slowed in exposed occupations.

2. Measuring "Observed Exposure"

Most exposure metrics focus on theoretical capability (e.g., Eloundou et al., 2023). However, there is a large gap between what an LLM *could* do and what is actually occurring due to legal constraints, human verification needs, and software hurdles.

Our new measure, Observed Exposure, combines data from O*NET, Eloundou et al., and real-world usage data from the Anthropic Economic Index. An occupation's exposure is higher if its tasks are performed in work-related contexts and heavily favor automated API implementation over augmentative (human-in-the-loop) usage.

3. The Most Exposed Occupations

Programmers are at the top, followed by Customer Service Representatives and Data Entry Keyers. Conversely, 30% of workers have zero coverage, including Cooks, Mechanics, and Lifeguards.

4. Worker Demographics

When comparing workers in the top quartile of AI exposure to those with zero exposure, a distinct demographic and economic profile emerges. The highly exposed workforce tends to be more educated and commands significantly higher compensation, earning roughly 47% more on average.

Furthermore, the demographic composition shifts notably: the most exposed group is 16 percentage points more likely to be female, 11 percentage points more likely to be white, and nearly twice as likely to be Asian compared to their unexposed counterparts.

5. Labor Market Outcomes

We focus on unemployment as our priority outcome because it most directly captures the potential for economic harm. Looking at Current Population Survey data since 2016, we compare workers in the top quartile of exposure to those with no exposure.

Result: During COVID, less-exposed workers saw a massive unemployment spike. Since the release of ChatGPT in late 2022, the trends between the two groups have been largely similar. The difference-in-differences estimate is indistinguishable from zero.

The Canary in the Coal Mine: Young Workers

While established workers aren't being laid off, hiring might be slowing. We analyzed the monthly job start rate for young workers (aged 22-25) entering new jobs. While unexposed jobs remained stable at ~2% inflow per month, entry into the most exposed jobs decreased.
